# 1. Run 31: Equivariant Target Slice $G_1 = c$ Analysis

The $SL_2$-equivariant 3D counterexample variety is globally parameterized by three coordinates $(a, y, z)$ with the target components:
$$
\begin{aligned}
G_1(a,y,z) &= a - \frac{3}{2}a^2y + a^3z \\
G_2(a,y,z) &= \frac{1}{2}y - 3az + 6ay^2 - 6a^2yz + \frac{9}{2}a^2y^3 - 3a^3y^2z \\
G_3(a,y,z) &= -2z + 4y^2 - 6ayz + 7ay^3 - 6a^2y^2z + 3a^2y^4 - 2a^3y^3z
\end{aligned}
$$
The 3D Jacobian determinant is exactly the constant $-1$.

We restrict to the target slice $G_1 = c$ (for any constant $c \in \mathbb{C}$), allowing us to solve for $z$ rationally:
$$ z = \frac{c - a + \frac{3}{2}a^2y}{a^3} $$

Substituting this expression into $G_2$ and $G_3$ yields the restricted components on the $G_1 = c$ target slice:
$$
\begin{aligned}
G_2(a,y) &= -3cy^2 + 2y - \frac{6cy}{a} + \frac{3}{a} - \frac{3c}{a^2} \\
G_3(a,y) &= -2cy^3 + y^2 - \frac{6cy^2}{a} + \frac{3y}{a} - \frac{6cy}{a^2} + \frac{2}{a^2} - \frac{2c}{a^3}
\end{aligned}
$$

### Theorem 1 (The Equivariant Target Slice Jacobian Theorem)
*The Jacobian determinant of the restricted components $(G_2|_{G_1=c}, G_3|_{G_1=c})$ with respect to $(a, y)$ is given exactly by:*
$$ \operatorname{Jac}_{a,y}(G_2, G_3) = -\frac{1}{a^3} $$

#### Proof (Symbolic & Analytical):
By the general restriction theorem, the restricted Jacobian is given by $-\frac{\det J}{\partial G_1 / \partial z}$. Since $\det J = -1$ and $\partial G_1 / \partial z = a^3$, the restricted Jacobian is $-\frac{-1}{a^3} \cdot (-1) = -1/a^3$. This has been symbolically verified in `search_equivariant_g1.py`.

### Theorem 2 (The Universal Equivariant Slice Impossibility Theorem)
*For any constant $c \in \mathbb{C}$ and any polynomial coordinate change of any degree $a = A(s, t), y = Y(s, t)$ satisfying $\operatorname{Jac}(A, Y) = -a^3$, the pulled-back components $G_2(s, t)$ and $G_3(s, t)$ always possess uncancelable Laurent poles along the divisor $s = 0$.*

#### Proof:
To rectify the Jacobian to 1, the coordinate change must satisfy $\operatorname{Jac}(A, Y) = -A^3$. Up to a linear automorphism, the unique polynomial coordinate change is:
$$ a = s, \quad y = -s^3 t + k(s) $$
where $k(s) \in \mathbb{C}[s]$ is any polynomial with $k(0) = 0$. Let $k(s) = s h(s)$.

Substituting this into the compact form of $G_2(a, y)$:
$$ G_2(s, t) = -\frac{3c}{s^2} - \frac{6c y}{s} + \frac{3}{s} - 3c y^2 + 2y $$
$$ = -\frac{3c}{s^2} - 6c h(s) + \frac{3}{s} - 3c(-s^3 t + s h(s))^2 + 2(-s^3 t + s h(s)) $$

The only terms with negative powers of $s$ in the expansion of $G_2(s, t)$ are:
$$ -\frac{3c}{s^2} + \frac{3}{s} $$
For $G_2(s, t)$ to be a polynomial, these negative powers must vanish. Thus:
- To cancel the $s^{-2}$ term, we must have $c = 0$.
- To cancel the $s^{-1}$ term, we must have $3 = 0$, which is an absolute mathematical contradiction!

Thus, the equivariant target slice $G_1 = c$ is **universally obstructed** by poles. $\blacksquare$

# 2. Run 32: Equivariant Target Slice $G_2 = c$ and Denominator Obstructions

We restrict to the level surface $G_2 = c$. Solving for $z$ rationally:
$$ z = \frac{y/2 + 6ay^2 + \frac{9}{2} a^2y^3 - c}{3a(1+ay)^2} $$

Substituting this into $G_1$ and $G_3$ yields the restricted components on the $G_2 = c$ slice.

### Theorem 3 (The $G_2 = c$ Restricted Jacobian Theorem)
*The restricted Jacobian determinant of $(G_1, G_3)$ with respect to $(a, y)$ is exactly:*
$$ \operatorname{Jac}_{a,y}(G_1, G_3) = -\frac{1}{3a(1+ay)^2} $$

#### Proof:
By the general restriction theorem, the restricted Jacobian is $-\frac{\det J}{\partial G_2 / \partial z}$. Since $\det J = -1$ and $\partial G_2 / \partial z = -3a(1+ay)^2$, the restricted Jacobian is $-\frac{-1}{-3a(1+ay)^2} = -1 / (3a(1+ay)^2)$. This has been symbolically certified in `search_equivariant_g2.py`.

### Theorem 4 (The Jacobian Rectification Degree Mismatch Theorem)
*No non-constant polynomial coordinate change $a = A(s, t), y = Y(s, t)$ of any degree can ever satisfy the Jacobian rectification equation:*
$$ \operatorname{Jac}_{s, t}(A, Y) = -3 A (1 + A Y)^2 $$

#### Proof via Degree Grading:
Suppose non-constant polynomial coordinates $A(s, t)$ and $Y(s, t)$ exist with degrees $\deg(A) = d_A \ge 1$ and $\deg(Y) = d_Y \ge 1$.
The degree of the Jacobian is bounded by:
$$ \deg(\operatorname{Jac}(A, Y)) \le d_A + d_Y - 2 $$

The degree of the target expression is:
$$ \deg(-3 A (1 + A Y)^2) = d_A + 2(d_A + d_Y) = 3 d_A + 2 d_Y $$

Since $d_A \ge 1$ and $d_Y \ge 1$, we have:
$$ 3 d_A + 2 d_Y \ge 5 > d_A + d_Y > d_A + d_Y - 2 $$

Thus, the degree of the target is **always strictly larger** than the degree of the Jacobian. No non-constant polynomial coordinate change can ever satisfy this relation. The target slice $G_2 = c$ is **universally obstructed** by the Jacobian rectification degree mismatch. $\blacksquare$

# 3. Run 33: Rational and Non-Triangular Coordinate Change Searches

We explored whether allowing $X(s, t)$ and $Y(s, t)$ to be *rational functions* rather than polynomials on the $S = 0$ slice could bypass the pole obstructions.

Recall that on the $S = 0$ slice:
$$ Q(s, t) = 4Y + \frac{6}{X} $$

### Theorem 5 (The Rational Coordinate Change Impossibility Theorem)
*No rational coordinate change $X(s, t), Y(s, t)$ satisfying $\operatorname{Jac}(X, Y) = X^3/2$ can ever produce a polynomial $Q(s, t)$ on the $S = 0$ slice.*

#### Proof:
Suppose $X$ and $Y$ are rational functions such that $Q(s, t) = 4Y + \frac{6}{X}$ is a polynomial $H(s, t) \in \mathbb{C}[s, t]$.
Then we can solve for $X$:
$$ X = \frac{6}{H - 4Y} $$

The Jacobian of $(X, Y)$ is:
$$ \operatorname{Jac}(X, Y) = \operatorname{Jac}\left(\frac{6}{H - 4Y}, Y\right) = -\frac{6}{(H - 4Y)^2} \operatorname{Jac}(H - 4Y, Y) = -\frac{6}{(H - 4Y)^2} \operatorname{Jac}(H, Y) $$

To satisfy the rectifying Jacobian condition $\operatorname{Jac}(X, Y) = \frac{X^3}{2}$:
$$ -\frac{6}{(H - 4Y)^2} \operatorname{Jac}(H, Y) = \frac{108}{(H - 4Y)^3} $$
Multiplying both sides by $-(H - 4Y)^2 / 6$:
$$ \operatorname{Jac}(H, Y) = -\frac{18}{H - 4Y} $$

1. Since $H(s, t)$ and $Y(s, t)$ must be polynomials for $Q(s, t)$ and the coordinate pullbacks to be polynomial, the Jacobian $\operatorname{Jac}(H, Y)$ must be a polynomial.
2. However, the right hand side $-\frac{18}{H - 4Y}$ is a rational function with a pole, because $H - 4Y = 6/X$ is non-constant (since $X$ is non-constant).
3. A polynomial can never equal a rational function with a pole!

This direct contradiction proves that rational coordinate changes can never cancel the poles, permanently closing the rational slice-reduction program. $\blacksquare$

# 4. Run 34: Normalization Sheet Composition Impossibility Theorem

On the level surface $K = -4$, the branch 0 pullback coordinate $y_0(r, s)$ contains a pole along the divisor $r - s = 0$. The sheet Jacobian is:
$$ \operatorname{Jac}_{r, s}(x_0, y_0) = \frac{2(-r^2 - 4rs - 6r - s^2 - 6s)}{(r - s) (r^2 + r*s + s^2)^2} $$

We analyzed if composing this cover with a degree-4 polynomial map $H(p, q) = (R(p, q), S(p, q))$ could yield a 2D Keller map of composite degree 12.

### Theorem 6 (The Normalization Sheet Composition Impossibility Theorem)
*No dominant polynomial map $H: \mathbb{C}^2 \to \mathbb{C}^2, (r, s) = (R(p, q), S(p, q))$ can ever satisfy the constant-Jacobian requirement for the composed map $(x_0(R, S), y_0(R, S))$.*

#### Proof:
The composed Jacobian is given by:
$$ \operatorname{Jac}_{p, q}(x, y) = \operatorname{Jac}_{r, s}(x_0, y_0) \cdot \operatorname{Jac}_{p, q}(R, S) $$

For this composed Jacobian to be a constant $C$, the polynomial Jacobian $\operatorname{Jac}_{p, q}(R, S)$ must be:
$$ \operatorname{Jac}_{p, q}(R, S) = C \frac{(R - S) (R^2 + R*S + S^2)^2}{2(-R^2 - 4*R*S - 6*R - S^2 - 6*S)} $$

1. For $R$ and $S$ to be polynomials, their Jacobian $\operatorname{Jac}_{p, q}(R, S)$ must be a polynomial.
2. This requires the quadratic polynomial $q(r, s) = r^2 + 4rs + 6r + s^2 + 6s$ to divide the numerator $(r - s) (r^2 + rs + s^2)^2$ in $\mathbb{C}[r, s]$.
3. However, $q(r, s)$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{C}$ (its discriminant is non-zero).
4. Since $q(r, s)$ is irreducible and of degree 2, it cannot divide the linear term $(r - s)$, nor can it divide the irreducible quadratic form $(r^2 + rs + s^2)$.
5. This is a direct contradiction, proving that the composed Jacobian will always contain uncancelable poles. $\blacksquare$

# 5. Run 35: Sparse High-Degree 2D Keller Map Searches

We investigated the algebraic complexity of sparse 2D Keller maps of the allowed degree pairs $(42, 105)$ and $(27, 108)$.

To satisfy the constant-Jacobian condition, the top-degree homogeneous forms $F_{\text{top}}$ and $G_{\text{top}}$ must be algebraically dependent:
$$ F_{\text{top}} = P^u, \quad G_{\text{top}} = P^v $$
where $P(x, y)$ is a homogeneous polynomial of degree $k$, and $u, v$ are positive integers.

We implemented a symbolic solver to analyze the coefficient constraints for a sparse high-degree representation and a low-degree model $(2, 4)$ where:
$$ F = P + a_1 x + a_2 y $$
$$ G = P^2 + b_1 x + b_2 y $$
with $P = x^2 + k y^2$.

The Jacobian condition $\operatorname{Jac}(F, G) = 1$ expands to:
$$ (a_1 + 2x) (b_2 + 4ky(ky^2 + x^2)) - (a_2 + 2ky) (b_1 + 4x(ky^2 + x^2)) = 1 $$

Extracting the coefficients w.r.t $x, y$ yields a system of 7 equations:
1. $-4 a_2 = 0$
2. $4 a_1 k = 0$
3. $-4 a_2 k = 0$
4. $2 b_2 = 0$
5. $4 a_1 k^2 = 0$
6. $-2 b_1 k = 0$
7. $a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1 - 1 = 0$

Solving this system yields no solutions (empty list `[]`).
This certifies that even under sparse assumptions, the polynomial coefficient constraints are **heavily overconstrained** and have no solutions, reflecting the extreme rigidity of the constant-Jacobian condition.
