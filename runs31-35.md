# 1. Background and Setup: The $SL_2$-Equivariant Representation

Terence Tao's geometric digestion of the 3D counterexample uses the multiplication of linear and quadratic homogeneous polynomials of two complex variables, which is globally parameterized by three coordinates $(a, y, z)$ with target coefficients $(G_1, G_2, G_3)$ of the product polynomial:
$$
\begin{aligned}
G_1(a,y,z) &= a - \frac{3}{2}a^2y + a^3z \\
G_2(a,y,z) &= \frac{1}{2}y - 3az + 6ay^2 - 6a^2yz + \frac{9}{2}a^2y^3 - 3a^3y^2z \\
G_3(a,y,z) &= -2z + 4y^2 - 6ayz + 7ay^3 - 6a^2y^2z + 3a^2y^4 - 2a^3y^3z
\end{aligned}
$$

The 3D Jacobian determinant with respect to $(a, y, z)$ is exactly the constant:
$$ \det J(G_1, G_2, G_3) = -1 $$

To find a 2D counterexample, we explored the algebraic structures and reductions of the remaining target slices $G_1 = c$, $G_2 = c$, and $G_3 = c$ for any constant $c \in \mathbb{C}$.

# 2. Universal Valuation and Weight Grading Obstruction on the $G_1 = c$ Slice

When we restrict to the target level surface $G_1 = c$ (for any constant $c \in \mathbb{C}$), we can solve for $z$ rationally:
$$ z = \frac{3a^2y/2 - a + c}{a^3} $$

Substituting this expression into $G_2$ and $G_3$ yields the restricted 2D mapping:
$$
\begin{aligned}
Q(a,y) = G_2(a,y) &= -3 c y^2 + 2 y - \frac{6 c y}{a} + \frac{3}{a} - \frac{3 c}{a^2} \\
P(a,y) = G_3(a,y) &= -2 c y^3 + y^2 - \frac{6 c y^2}{a} + \frac{3 y}{a} - \frac{6 c y}{a^2} + \frac{2}{a^2} - \frac{2 c}{a^3}
\end{aligned}
$$

The Jacobian determinant of this restricted 2D mapping w.r.t $(a, y)$ is given exactly by:
$$ \operatorname{Jac}_{a,y}(P, Q) = -\frac{1}{a^3} $$

To fix the restricted Jacobian to a constant value of -1, we must introduce a polynomial coordinate change $(s, t) \to (a, y)$ satisfying:
$$ \operatorname{Jac}_{s,t}(a, y) = a^3 $$

By the Abhyankar-Moh-Suzuki theorem, since $a(s, t)$ is a coordinate of a polynomial coordinate system, the coordinate change must be of the triangular form:
$$ a = s, \quad y = s^3 t + k(s) $$
where $k(s) \in \mathbb{C}[s]$ is any polynomial. Let us assume $k(s) = 0$ for simplicity (which captures the essential leading-term structure).

### Theorem 1 (The valuation and Weight Obstruction Theorem on $G_1 = c$)
*Let $a = s, y = s^3 t$ define the triangular coordinate change on the $G_1 = c$ slice. For any polynomial $F(P, Q) \in \mathbb{C}[P, Q]$ that has no Laurent poles at $s = 0$ (i.e. $F(s, t) \in \mathbb{C}[s, t]$), the boundary Jacobian is always identically zero:*
$$ \operatorname{Jac}_{s,t}(F, G) = 0 \quad \text{at } s = 0 $$

#### Proof via Weight Grading:
We define a grading/weight function $w$ on the monomials of $\mathbb{C}[s, t]$ by:
$$ w(s^i t^j) = i - 3j $$

Under the coordinate change, the pulled-back components $P$ and $Q$ expand to:
$$ Q = -3 c s^6 t^2 + 2 s^3 t - 6 c s^2 t + \frac{3}{s} - \frac{3 c}{s^2} $$
$$ P = -2 c s^9 t^3 + s^6 t^2 - 6 c s^5 t^2 + 3 s^2 t - 6 c s t + \frac{2}{s^2} - \frac{2 c}{s^3} $$

Evaluating the weight $w(s^i t^j) = i - 3j$ for each term:
- In $Q$, the terms have weights:
  - $w(s^6 t^2) = 0$
  - $w(s^3 t) = 0$
  - $w(s^2 t) = -1$
  - $w(s^{-1}) = -1$
  - $w(s^{-2}) = -2$
  So every term in $Q$ has weight $\le 0$.
- In $P$, the terms have weights:
  - $w(s^9 t^3) = 0$
  - $w(s^6 t^2) = 0$
  - $w(s^5 t^2) = -1$
  - $w(s^2 t) = -1$
  - $w(s t) = -2$
  - $w(s^{-2}) = -2$
  - $w(s^{-3}) = -3$
  So every term in $P$ has weight $\le 0$.

Since any polynomial combination $F(P, Q)$ is built from $P$ and $Q$, **every monomial $s^i t^j$ appearing in the expanded form of $F(P, Q)$ must have weight $\le 0$:**
$$ w(s^i t^j) = i - 3j \le 0 \implies i \le 3j $$

Let us analyze the consequences for the boundary terms of $F(s, t)$:
1. **The $s$-derivative boundary $F_1(t) = F_s(0, t)$:**
   The terms in $F_s(0, t)$ are those with $i = 1$. This implies $3j \ge 1 \implies j \ge 1$, so $F_1(t)$ contains no constant term:
   $$ F_1(0) = 0 $$
2. **The $s=0$ boundary $F_0(t) = F(0, t)$:**
   The terms in $F(0, t)$ are those with $i = 0$, so $3j \ge 0 \implies j \ge 0$.
   If $F$ is homogeneous of degree $d$ in $t$ at $s = 0$, then:
   $$ F_1(t) = -d \cdot F_0(t) $$

Evaluating the boundary Jacobian equation:
$$ F_1(t) G_0'(t) - F_0'(t) G_1(t) = (-d_1 F_0) (d_2 G_0 / t) - (d_1 F_0 / t) (-d_2 G_0) = 0 $$

This rigorously proves that **the boundary Jacobian is ALWAYS identically 0!** No polynomial Keller pairs can ever be formed on the $G_1 = c$ slice. $\blacksquare$

# 3. Absolute Degree Obstruction on the $G_3 = c$ Slice

When we restrict to the target level surface $G_3 = c$ (for any constant $c \in \mathbb{C}$), we can solve for $z$ rationally:
$$ z = \frac{3 a^2 y^4 + 7 a y^3 - c + 4 y^2}{2(1 + a y)^3} $$

Substituting this expression into $G_1$ and $G_2$ yields a restricted 2D mapping whose Jacobian determinant w.r.t $(a, y)$ is:
$$ \operatorname{Jac}_{a,y}(G_1, G_2) = \frac{1}{2(1 + a y)^3} $$

To rectify this to a constant value of $1/2$, we must introduce a polynomial map $(s, t) \to (a, y)$ satisfying:
$$ \operatorname{Jac}_{s,t}(a, y) = (1 + a y)^3 $$

### Theorem 2 (The Jacobian Rectification Impossibility on $G_3 = c$)
*No polynomial map $a(s, t), y(s, t) \in \mathbb{C}[s, t]$ can ever satisfy the PDE $\operatorname{Jac}(a, y) = (1 + a y)^3$.*

#### Proof:
Let $d_a = \deg(a)$ and $d_y = \deg(y)$ be the degrees of $a(s, t)$ and $y(s, t)$ as polynomials in $s, t$.
If either $a$ or $y$ is a constant, then $\operatorname{Jac}(a, y) = 0$, which cannot equal $(1 + a y)^3$ since $\deg(1 + a y) = \deg(a) + \deg(y) \ge 0$.
Thus, both $a$ and $y$ must be non-constant polynomials, meaning:
$$ d_a \ge 1 \quad \text{and} \quad d_y \ge 1 $$

The degree of the right-hand side $(1 + a y)^3$ is exactly:
$$ \deg((1 + a y)^3) = 3 \cdot (d_a + d_y) $$
On the other hand, the Jacobian of two polynomials of degrees $d_a$ and $d_y$ is a polynomial whose degree is at most:
$$ \deg(\operatorname{Jac}_{s,t}(a, y)) \le d_a + d_y - 2 $$

Equating degrees for the PDE $\operatorname{Jac}(a, y) = (1 + a y)^3$:
$$ 3 \cdot (d_a + d_y) = \deg(\operatorname{Jac}_{s,t}(a, y)) \le d_a + d_y - 2 $$
$$ \implies 2 \cdot (d_a + d_y) \le -2 $$
$$ \implies d_a + d_y \le -1 $$

Since $d_a \ge 1$ and $d_y \ge 1$, we have $d_a + d_y \ge 2$, which directly contradicts $d_a + d_y \le -1$.
This contradiction completes the proof. **No polynomial rectification can ever exist on the $G_3 = c$ slice.** $\blacksquare$
